01
TVC Demonstrator

A scaled thrust vector control system designed from blank sheet — 2-DOF gimbal mechanism, custom PCB, servo actuation, and closed-loop IMU feedback. Diagnosed gravitationally induced sensor error and derived a physics-based correction.

Onshape CADPCB DesignESP32 PID ControlIMU FusionServo Actuation
+
Overview

The TVC Demonstrator is a self-directed project modeling the core mechanism behind rocket engine steering — a two-degree-of-freedom gimbal that redirects thrust to control attitude. The entire system was designed from scratch with no pre-existing template: mechanism geometry derived analytically, PCB laid out in EasyEDA, firmware written for ESP32, and all structural components fabricated by hand.

Key Engineering Challenge

During closed-loop testing, yaw and pitch axes showed asymmetric behavior that could not be explained by mechanical misalignment. Systematic analysis revealed the IMU mounting location — offset from the gimbal center at radius r = 0.0674 m — introduced centripetal acceleration (ω²r, mapped to X-axis) and tangential acceleration (αr, mapped to Z-axis) that corrupted the orientation estimate differently for each rotation axis. A physics-based correction was derived and implemented, with validation performed through a custom real-time oscilloscope dashboard that measured the noise floor before and after correction.

02
Cortisol Biosensor — MSc Thesis

Designed and fabricated a molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) based electrochemical biosensor for non-invasive cortisol detection in sweat and saliva. Validated across sensitivity, selectivity, and 23-day stability. Led to a first-author publication in Microchemical Journal (Elsevier, 2026).

ElectrochemistryMIP SynthesisCyclic Voltammetry BiosensingPalmSens4Published
+
Overview

Cortisol is the primary biomarker of physiological stress and plays a central role in metabolic regulation, immune function, and circadian rhythm. Accurate real-time detection without invasive blood draws remains an unsolved clinical challenge. This project developed a molecularly imprinted polymer (MIP) based sensor on screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs) that detects cortisol electrochemically from sweat or saliva at concentrations spanning 100 pM to 1 µM — a range clinically relevant to stress monitoring.

Sensor Design and Fabrication

The sensor uses electropolymerization of polypyrrole on screen-printed carbon electrodes to create a molecularly imprinted recognition layer selective to cortisol. Cortisol molecules are embedded during polymerization and removed via overoxidation, leaving complementary cavities that selectively rebind cortisol from solution. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and differential pulse voltammetry (DPV) were used for electrochemical characterization. All fabrication and testing was performed in the MNT Group laboratory at UPC Barcelona.

Results

The calibration curve achieved R²=0.992 across a 100 pM to 1 µM cortisol range with a logarithmic fit, confirming consistent sensor response across the clinically relevant detection window. Selectivity testing against structurally similar interferents — lactate, prednisolone, and progesterone — demonstrated that lactate produced negligible cross-response while steroid interferents showed measurable but distinguishable signals. SEM imaging confirmed distinct surface morphology differences between bare electrodes, NIP controls, and MIP-coated sensors.

PIV Fluid Dynamics Research

Designed and built a complete Particle Image Velocimetry system from scratch to study laminar-to-turbulent flow transition. Validated against two independent theoretical benchmarks: Torricelli's Law (R²=0.958) and projectile motion theory (R²=0.985).

PIV / PIVlabMATLABFusion 360 CAD CNC MachiningLaser OpticsFluid Dynamics
+
Overview

This undergraduate research project at Wisconsin Lutheran College developed a validated PIV measurement system for quantitative fluid flow analysis. The entire experimental apparatus — Torricelli panel, optical bench, tracer particle delivery — was designed in Fusion 360 and fabricated using CNC machining and laser cutting. PIVlab (MATLAB) was used for cross-correlation velocity extraction from 2,300 frames per run.

Key Results
  • PIV velocity data agreed with projectile motion model (R² = 0.985)
  • Torricelli's Law trendline yielded R² = 0.958 across all heights
  • Measured mean velocity range: 0.00 – 0.22 m/s at 0 – 35 mm head
  • Beam profile characterised: divergence 1.1 mRad, convergence 1.2 mRad
  • Two interchangeable nozzle geometries fabricated and tested
Instrumentation
  • LaserCube 2W 532 nm — knife-edge beam profiled
  • Thorlabs optical mounts and precision alignment rails
  • DSLR high-speed capture for particle tracking
  • PIVlab (MATLAB) cross-correlation velocity extraction
  • Optical microscope for tracer particle sizing

06
Self-Balancing Robot

Two-wheeled inverted pendulum robot built from scratch — laser-cut chassis, 3D-printed wheels, NEMA 17 steppers, and a Kalman-filtered PID loop on Arduino. Achieved stable balance after three full redesigns and systematic resolution of cascading hardware and firmware failures.

Arduino / C++PID ControlKalman Filter MPU-6050NEMA 17 SteppersLaser Cut
+
Overview

Built with a collaborator over an iterative series of sessions, this project pushed through three complete chassis redesigns and multiple hardware-level failures. The final build uses 2A NEMA 17 steppers driven by an Adafruit Motor Shield V2, a Kalman-filtered IMU for angle estimation, and a tuned PID controller (Kp=39.5, Ki=2.2, Kd=0.11) that achieves stable, repeatable balance. I²C was increased to 400 kHz to reduce motor latency below the control loop's stability threshold.

Failures Overcome
  • Initial 350mA steppers lacked torque — replaced with 2A NEMA 17s
  • PWM timing conflicts between motor library and IMU polling — resolved through interrupt restructuring
  • Asynchronous stepping caused resonance oscillation — fixed with coordinated step sequencing
  • I²C at 100 kHz introduced latency that destabilised PID — increased to 400 kHz

Quadcopter UAV

Custom quadcopter built from components across mechanical, electrical, and firmware layers. Resolved layered hardware, RF interference, and firmware failures. Built a Python WiFi telemetry console for real-time in-flight PID tuning.

ESP32 / CESP-IDF v4.4Brushless Motors BLHeli_S ESCKalman FilterWiFi UDP
+
Overview

Built collaboratively around an ESP32-WROOM-32 flight controller, 4-in-1 BLHeli_S 30A ESC, and iFlight XING-E Pro 2306 brushless motors. The system required integration across mechanical, electrical, and software layers simultaneously. A key diagnostic challenge was the ADC2/WiFi co-interference on the ESP32, which required all PWM outputs to be mapped to ADC1 pins.

Firmware and Software

Flight firmware written in C using ESP-IDF v4.4. Core tasks include IMU data acquisition at I²C 400 kHz, Kalman / complementary filter for angle estimation, PID stabilisation loop, Bluepad32 gamepad polling, and WiFi UDP telemetry transmission. The companion Python script (MBL_QTC) provides a live telemetry console for real-time PID tuning.

06
Rubens' Tube

Designed and fabricated a standing wave visualization apparatus from scratch. Applied fluid dynamics principles to achieve stable, repeatable flame patterns deployable across multiple resonant frequency modes — built as a lecture demonstration tool.

AcousticsFluid DynamicsFabricationStanding Waves
+
Concept

A Rubens' tube demonstrates that standing acoustic waves produce stationary pressure nodes and antinodes. Flame height is proportional to local gas pressure amplitude — meaning the standing wave pattern becomes directly visible as a stable landscape of flame heights. By sweeping the drive frequency, multiple harmonics become observable as distinct, repeatable patterns. The tube was designed and fabricated to serve as a teaching instrument, optimising for stability and repeatability of the flame patterns rather than purely visual spectacle.

About

The person
behind the work

I grew up taking things apart to understand how they worked — alarm clocks, computers, anything with moving parts. That instinct led me to physics, which forces you to build understanding from first principles rather than just applying formulas, and then to engineering, where that understanding becomes something physical you can hold.

I completed my MSc in Engineering Physics at the Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya in Barcelona, where I developed a molecularly imprinted polymer biosensor for non-invasive cortisol detection — work that led to a first-author publication in Microchemical Journal (Elsevier, 2026). Before that I spent four years at Wisconsin Lutheran College, where I simultaneously ran fluid dynamics research, taught AP Physics, and worked as a cabinet maker to fund my degree.

Outside of engineering I shoot with a Sony Alpha, play and compose music, and build things with friends — the quadcopter and self-balancing robot on this page were both collaborative projects born from curiosity rather than obligation. I lived in Barcelona for 18 months and speak Spanish fluently. I moved there on a one-way ticket and figured the rest out as I went.
